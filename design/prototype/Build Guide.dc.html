Flight Board · Build Guide Draft one
BUILD GUIDE · FOR SOMEONE WHO HAS NEVER SHIPPED AN APP

Flight Board, from design to a round played for real

You have finished the part most first-time builders never finish. Every screen is decided, every edge case has an answer, and the copy is written. What remains is translation, and you are doing it with Claude Code — which means your real skill is not typing code, it is describing precisely what you already designed and knowing when what comes back is wrong.

Read this once end to end before you start. Then work one phase at a time, in order. Each phase ends at something you can hold and show somebody, on purpose.

The honest shape of this

Expect eight to twelve weeks of evenings to reach a round played by real golfers, and a few months beyond that for a 300-player tournament. The live-scoring part is easier than it sounds — that problem is solved by tools you will not have to write. The genuinely hard parts, in order: getting a build onto a physical iPhone, handling a phone that loses signal on the back nine, and the unglamorous question of state — who may change which number, and when.

THE ONE RULE

Build the smallest thing a real person can use, then put it in front of a real person. The pull toward building the tournament layer first will be strong — you designed for 300 players. Resist it. One foursome at Gladstan will teach you more than three months of scaffolding.

What you will build it with

Four pieces. You do not need to understand them deeply yet — you need to know what each is for, so you can tell whose fault a problem is.

Piece Job Why this one
Expo

Phase 0 · Setup

About an hour. Do it in one sitting — it removes every later excuse.

01Open the accounts. GitHub, Supabase, GolfCourseAPI, Expo. All free. Put every password and key into one password-manager entry now.
02Install the tools. Node.js (the LTS version), Git, VS Code, and Claude Code. If an install fails, paste the error straight into Claude Code — diagnosing setup errors is one of the things it is genuinely excellent at.
03Install Expo Go on your phone. This is the app that runs your app while you build. Getting your own phone showing your own code is the moment this stops feeling theoretical.
04Create the project and put the design in it. Ask Claude Code to create a new Expo app with expo-router, initialise a Git repository, and push it to GitHub. Then export this project's prototype and drop it in a design/ folder inside it, so Claude Code can read the actual screens rather than your description of them.
DONE WHEN

Your phone displays a blank screen with your own text on it, and changing that text on your laptop changes it on your phone within a second or two.

Phase 1 · One screen, no backend

A week or two of evenings.

Build the hole-by-hole score entry screen and nothing else. No sign-in, no database, no other players — four hard-coded names and a score you can change with the plus and minus buttons. The numbers reset every time you reload, and that is fine.

This is the right first screen because it is the one that has to be perfect. It is also where you learn what "state" means in practice: the score lives in memory, the buttons change it, the screen redraws. Everything else you build is a variation on that loop.

Take it to a course and enter a fake round on the walk. In sunlight, with one hand, wearing a glove. You will find two or three things the design got wrong that no amount of desk review would have surfaced.

DONE WHEN

You can walk eighteen holes tapping in your own score, and the running total is right at the end.

Phase 2 · Sign in, and scores that survive

Two to three weeks. The steepest part of the climb.

Now connect Supabase. Phone-number sign-in with a texted code is a feature it provides directly — you configure it rather than build it. Then design the database: rounds, players, groups, holes, scores. Ask Claude Code to propose the tables from your prototype's screens, then read the proposal back and check it against reality. If it cannot express "one scorer entering for four people", it is wrong.

Two things here will cost you real money if you get them wrong, so slow down for both. First, row-level security — the rules that stop a player editing somebody else's score. Get these right before anyone but you uses the app. Second, SMS costs: every sign-in code and every invite is a paid text. At 300 players that is a real number. Check it before you promise a tournament to anyone.

DONE WHEN

You sign in with your real phone number, enter a score, force-quit the app, reopen it, and the score is still there.

Phase 3 · Live

A week. The most satisfying week.

Turn on Supabase realtime so a score posted on one phone appears on another. Build the group leaderboard. Then the moment worth waiting for: put the app on two phones, hand one to somebody, and watch a number you type appear in their hand. That is the product. Everything else is around it.

Add both scoring modes here, because they are the same data seen two ways — everyone entering their own, or one scorer entering for the group.

DONE WHEN

Two phones, one round, and neither person ever presses refresh.

Phase 4 · A whole round, start to signature

Two to three weeks. This is the MVP.

Fill in the path around the middle: create a round, search the course and auto-fill the holes (with the favourites-and-cache tiers from the design), add players by phone number or from contacts, send the invite link, and — the one that makes this replace paper — the final scorecard and digital sign-off.

Two smaller pieces belong here because they are load-bearing rather than decorative. The bottom navigation — Score, Board, Games — is what the app actually is once a round is live; build it before you have three screens to move between, not after. And the first-run name screen, one step after the texted code, because every card, board and signature depends on a name existing.

Offline belongs here too, and it is the hardest thing in the build. The rule to hold onto: the phone writes the score locally first and always succeeds, then syncs when it can. Never make a golfer wait on a network to record a four. Tell Claude Code that rule explicitly and in those words — it changes how the whole data layer gets written, and retrofitting it later is genuinely painful.

DONE WHEN

A real foursome plays a real round at Gladstan on their own phones, in aeroplane mode for part of it, and all four sign their cards at the end. Buy them a drink and listen to everything they complain about.

Phase 5 · The tournament layer

Four to six weeks, and only after phase 4 has survived contact.

Everything you designed and deferred: bulk roster import, groups at scale, shotgun and double-shotgun starting-hole assignment with A/B lettering, flights, the full-field board and tapping into any player's round, day-of no-shows and swaps, teams and the re-draw at the turn, the spectator web view, and the spreadsheet export.

Side games deserve their own week inside this phase, because they are three different shapes of money wearing one label. Hole games (closest to the pin, longest drive) run on several holes at once and pay from a field-wide pot. Wolf settles person-to-person every hole, off a rotation that must be reshufflable. Team challenge settles team-to-team across three separate wagers. Build Wolf first — if your data model can express Wolf, it can express the other two.

The settle-up screen is where these converge, and it is the one place a bug is embarrassing in front of people holding cash. It nets every game down to the fewest payments that clear the group, and it deliberately excludes the pot games. Test it with lopsided numbers before you trust it.

One new technical problem appears at this size and it is worth naming now: three hundred phones all watching one leaderboard is a different load than four. Expect to batch updates rather than push every single score to every single phone the instant it lands. Do not solve it in advance — but do not be surprised by it either.

DONE WHEN

A club scramble runs on it and the pro shop asks to use it again.

Phase 6 · The stores

Three to four weeks, mostly waiting.

Apple charges 99 dollars a year, Google 25 dollars once. You will need an icon, screenshots, a privacy policy, and a description. Two specific things to prepare for, given what this app does:

The wager question

Both stores scrutinise anything that looks like gambling. Your design is already on the right side of this — Flight Board tracks who owes whom and moves no money — but say so plainly in the review notes, and keep it that way. The day you add payments you are in a different category of app with a different review.

The contacts permission

Apple wants a clear reason. You already wrote it, in the picker sheet: names and mobile numbers only, only while the sheet is open, only for the ones you tap. Use that sentence.

Working with Claude Code

This is the part that decides whether the plan works. Six habits, in rough order of how much they matter.

01Give it the design, not a description of the designKeep the prototype in the repo and point at it: "build this screen, matching design/ exactly." Every hour spent describing a layout in prose is an hour spent badly.
02One screen per conversation"Build the app" produces something impressive that you do not understand and cannot fix. "Build the score entry screen, no backend yet, four hard-coded players" produces something you can read. Small requests, finished and tested, then the next one.
03Commit every time something worksAsk it to commit to Git after each working piece. When a change breaks everything — and it will — you can return to the last good state instead of unpicking it. This single habit will save you a weekend.
04Make it explain before it builds"Before you write anything, tell me in plain English what you are about to do and why." You are the one who knows how golf works. If the plan says a score belongs to a group rather than a player, you will catch it in the plan and not three screens later.
05Paste errors wholeThe entire red block, not your summary of it. Then say what you were doing when it appeared. Never apologise for not understanding an error — that is what the error is for.
06Write down the rules that must not driftKeep a CLAUDE.md file at the top of the project holding the decisions that apply everywhere. Claude Code reads it every session, so you stop re-explaining and it stops forgetting. The current list is on the next page — paste it in as-is.
WHEN YOU ARE STUCK FOR MORE THAN AN HOUR

Stop and ask it to explain the problem to you as if you had never seen code, then ask for the two simplest options and their trade-offs. Beginners lose whole weekends to a problem that had an easy answer nobody offered them. You are allowed to ask for the easy answer.

The rules the design already decided

These came out of building the prototype, and several were arrived at by getting them wrong first. Put them in CLAUDE.md verbatim.

Scores write locally first, always

The write succeeds on the phone and syncs later. Never make a golfer wait on a network to record a four.

A player edits only their own score

Unless they are the group's designated scorer. Any hole can be disputed for five minutes after it posts.

One number, one source

Never store a figure that can be derived. The wolf for a hole is computed from the rotation, not saved against the hole; a player's card is derived from their standing; per-nine totals are summed from that nine. Every duplicate became a contradiction on screen.

Never show a score for a hole that has not been played

Unplayed holes are blank. Withdrawals show WD. A round in progress has no net or Stableford total.

Say what a number is

"Thru 6" only on tee times; on a shotgun it is "6 played". A pot is not a per-team exposure. Label each one for what it actually represents.

The app never moves money

It tracks who owes whom and nets it to the fewest payments. Field-pot games settle at turn-in and stay out of the group tally.

Rotations must be reshufflable

Wolf order, starting holes, team draws. Anything fixed hands the same player the same advantage every week at the same course.

A signed card is locked

Reopening it takes the organizer. Every day-of change is logged with the name of whoever made it.

Where first-timers actually get stuck

Getting a real build onto an iPhone

Expo Go covers you for months. The day you need a proper installable build, Apple's certificates and provisioning profiles are a genuinely miserable afternoon. Set that afternoon aside and do not schedule it the day before a tournament.

Two people scoring the same hole

A player enters a five while the group scorer enters a six. Decide the rule now, before it happens: last write wins, the scorer wins, or the app flags it and asks. Your design already has a five-minute dispute window — build to that.

Testing alone

A multiplayer app cannot be tested by one person. Keep an old phone, or a simulator, or a patient friend permanently on call. From phase 3 onward you need at least two devices to know anything at all.

Building the fun parts first

Wolf and the side games are more fun to build than sign-in. Build sign-in.

Not the next three months. Three hours, this week:
